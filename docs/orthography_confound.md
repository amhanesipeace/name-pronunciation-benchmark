# Robustness: is the bias just an exotic-spelling effect?

Generated by `orthography_confound.py` from the committed scores (gTTS isolated, the clean-baseline run). Orthographic markers = letter patterns rare in English given-name spelling (digraphs gb/kp/kw/gw/nw/ny, a word-initial syllabic nasal, or a 3+ vowel run); `ch`/`sh` are **not** counted because English names use them.

**1. Do African names carry more non-English spelling markers?**

- English: mean 0.00 markers/name.
- African: mean 0.08 markers/name.
- African > English: Mann-Whitney U **p = 9.48e-02** (no significant difference — these African names are, on the page, almost as plain as the English set).

**2. Within African names, does marker count predict CER?**

- Spearman ρ = -0.002 (p = 9.89e-01, n = 60).
- Spelling markers do **not** predict how badly a name is rendered.

**3. Does the gap survive among orthographically *simple* names?**

- Names with zero markers: English n = 20, African n = 55.
- Mean CER among these plain-spelled names: English 0.006 vs African 0.374.
- African > English CER among simple names: **p = 2.10e-08** (gap persists).

**Conclusion.** Non-English spelling markers are rare even in the African set and do not predict CER, yet among names written with ordinary letter patterns the full English–African gap remains. The disparity is not driven by exotic orthography — plain-spelled African names (Oluwaseun, Adebayo, Folake) are mispronounced at the same rate — but by the system's familiarity with the name and its language.
